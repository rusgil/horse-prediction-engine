How our ratings work

Every runner we rate gets a single headline number: a win probability. This guide explains what that number really means, the signals behind it, and how we use it to look for value rather than just favourites.

What a win probability means

A win probability is an estimate of how often a horse would win if the same race were run many times over. If we rate a horse a 25% chance, we're saying that in a large number of similar races, it would win about one in four. It is not a prediction that this horse will win today — no honest model can promise that. Racing is genuinely uncertain, and even a strong favourite loses most of the time once you account for the whole field.

Because every runner's chance is expressed on the same 0–100% scale, the numbers are directly comparable across horses, races and meetings. A 25% chance in one race means the same thing as a 25% chance in another.

The signals behind the number

The model reads the same information a sharp form analyst would — just across every runner in every race, without fatigue or favouritism. The main signal groups are:

  • Form and class: recent finishing positions, the quality of the races they were earned in, and how the horse is trending.
  • Suitability: distance, track condition and venue — is this horse well-placed for this race, or out of its comfort zone?
  • Barrier and likely run: the draw in the context of the distance and track shape (see our barrier guide).
  • Connections: trainer and jockey records, including their strike rates in relevant conditions.
  • Pedigree: breeding signals, which matter most for lightly-raced and first-up horses with little form to go on.
  • The market: the betting market is a strong independent estimate, and we treat it as a serious input rather than ignoring it.

Calibration: making the numbers honest

A probability is only useful if it's calibrated — meaning that horses rated a 20% chance actually win about 20% of the time over a large sample. A model that spits out confident-looking numbers that don't hold up is worse than useless. We continually check our ratings against real results and adjust so the numbers stay honest. When you see a probability, it's meant to be taken literally, not as marketing.

Finding value, not just winners

Here's the part that separates rating a race from betting well. Backing the most likely winner is not automatically profitable — if a horse is a genuine 50% chance but the market only pays $1.80, there's no value in the price. Value exists when our estimate of a horse's chance is higher than the chance implied by its odds.

So the model's real job isn't to tell you the favourite; the market already does that, and usually does it well. Its job is to find the runners where the price looks generous relative to their true chance — the overlays. That's a harder and more valuable thing to do, and it's where a disciplined model can add something a quick look at the odds can't.

Odds imply a probability. Divide 1 by the decimal odds to get the market's implied chance: $5.00 ≈ 20%, $2.50 ≈ 40%. Compare that to our estimate. When ours is meaningfully higher, the price is potentially value; when it's lower, the horse is likely too short.

Reading our Sharp and Edge markers

We surface two things beyond the raw probability:

  • Sharp is a quality filter. It flags the selections that clear a stricter bar — where the model is more confident and the profile is cleaner. A quiet day with few Sharp selections is by design: the filter is meant to hold a standard, not fill a page.
  • Edge highlights where our estimate and the market disagree in our favour — the value angle above. It's about the price, not just the chance of winning.

Use it as one input, not gospel

A model is a tool for thinking, not a guarantee. The sensible way to use ratings is to let them focus your attention — surface the value, flag the overlooked — and then apply your own judgement and, above all, your own staking discipline. No rating changes the fact that racing carries risk. Treat any wager as money you can afford to lose, and if it stops being fun, stop.
