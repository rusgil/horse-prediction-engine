Reference

Australian racing glossary

The words and abbreviations you'll meet on a race card and in commentary, defined in plain English. Terms are grouped so related ideas sit together rather than being scattered alphabetically.

The race itself

Meeting
A single day's racing at one venue — for example, "the Randwick meeting". A meeting is made up of several individual races, usually numbered R1 through to R8 or R9.
Card
The full list of races and runners for a meeting. "Reading the card" means working through the fields to form a view.
Field
The set of horses entered to run in a particular race. A "big field" (say 14–16 runners) is harder to win than a small one.
Distance
How far the race is run, in metres — from sprints around 1000m to staying races of 2400m and beyond. Distance shapes tactics, which horses are suited, and how much the barrier draw matters.
Class
The grade of a race, reflecting the quality of horses eligible. Ratings-band races (e.g. Benchmark 64), Maidens, and Group/Listed black-type races sit on a ladder from lowest to highest class. A horse "rising in class" meets tougher opponents.
Maiden
A race restricted to horses that have never won. A "maiden" is also the horse itself — one still seeking a first career victory.
Group and Listed races
The elite tier, collectively "black-type" races. Group 1 is the pinnacle, then Group 2, Group 3, then Listed. Winning or placing in them is what makes a horse valuable at stud.
Handicap
A race in which horses carry different weights, assigned by a handicapper to give every runner a theoretically equal chance. Better horses carry more.
Weight-for-age (WFA)
A weight scale based on a horse's age and sex rather than ability, so the best horses meet on near-level terms. WFA races are where champions are truly tested.
Set weights
A race where all horses carry the same weight, sometimes with allowances for sex or previous wins.

The runner

Barrier (draw)
The starting gate a horse is allocated, numbered from the inside (rail) outward. See our barrier draw guide for why it matters.
Weight
The total the horse must carry — jockey plus saddle and lead weights — to meet its handicap. Listed in kilograms on the card.
Jockey
The rider. An apprentice is a jockey still in training who can "claim" — carry less weight as an allowance — which can offset a horse's handicap.
Trainer
The person responsible for preparing the horse. Stable form, and a trainer's record at a track or distance, are meaningful signals.
Gear changes
Equipment added or removed for a run — blinkers, winkers, tongue-tie, ear muffs, cross-over noseband. A first-time gear change can sharpen a horse's focus or settle it, and is often noted as significant.
Blinkers
Eye cups that narrow a horse's vision to keep it focused forward. "First-time blinkers" is a classic tried-something-new angle.
Colours (silks)
The registered jacket and cap the jockey wears, identifying the owner.
Gelding
A castrated male horse. Gelding often improves temperament and focus; a recent gelding operation can trigger a form turnaround.
Colt / Filly / Mare / Entire
A colt is a young entire (uncastrated) male; a filly a young female; a mare an older female; an entire an uncastrated male of any age.

Form and fitness

Form (the form line)
A horse's recent finishing positions, read left to right, oldest to most recent — so "3-1-2" means third, then a win, then second last start. An x or dash marks a spell (a break from racing).
Spell
An extended rest from racing, typically several months. First-up (first run back from a spell) and second-up records tell you how a horse performs fresh.
First-up / Second-up
A horse's first or second run back from a spell. Some horses win fresh; others need a run or two to reach peak fitness.
Let-up
A shorter break than a spell — a few weeks off — without a full freshen.
Barrier trial
An unofficial practice race with no betting, used to educate young horses or tune fitness. Trial form can hint at readiness before it shows in official results.
Scratching
The withdrawal of a horse from a race before it runs. Late scratchings change the field, the barrier map, and the market.
First starter / Debutant
A horse having its very first race start, with no form to read — only trials, breeding and market signals.

The track

Going (track rating)
The condition of the surface, rated 1–10 from Firm to Heavy. Full detail in our track conditions guide.
Rail position
How far the running rail has been moved out from its true position, in metres. Moving the rail out spreads wear and can change where the best going is.
Straight / Home turn
The final straight is the run to the finish line; the home turn is the last bend before it. Wide runs around the home turn cost ground.
Sectional times
The time taken for segments of a race (e.g. the last 600m). Fast closing sectionals can flatter or expose a run depending on tempo.
Track bias
A tendency on a given day for one part of the track — the inside rail, or on-pace runners — to be favoured by the prevailing conditions.

Betting

Odds (price)
What a bookmaker will pay on a winning bet. Shorter odds (e.g. $2.00) mean a more favoured runner; longer odds (e.g. $21) a less favoured one.
Favourite
The runner with the shortest odds — the horse the market rates most likely to win.
SP (Starting Price)
The odds at the moment the race starts. Bets can be taken at fixed odds earlier or at SP.
Tote / Pari-mutuel
A pool betting system where all bets go into a pool and the dividend depends on how much was bet on the winner. Contrasts with fixed-odds.
Win / Place
A win bet pays only if your horse finishes first. A place bet pays if it finishes in the top two or three (depending on field size), at shorter odds.
Each-way
Two bets in one — a win bet and a place bet on the same horse — so you collect something if it places even without winning.
Overlay / Underlay
An overlay is a horse whose odds are longer than its true chance suggests — potential value. An underlay is the opposite: shorter than it should be.
Value
Betting value exists when the odds on offer are longer than the true probability of winning. Backing winners is not the same as betting well; long-term profit comes from value, not from picking favourites.
Exotics
Bets on multiple runners or races — quinella (first two in any order), exacta (first two in exact order), trifecta (first three in order), First Four, and quaddie (winners of four nominated races).
Multi
A single bet combining several selections, all of which must win for it to pay — higher risk, higher reward.
A note on the numbers. Odds imply a probability: a $2.00 chance is roughly a 50% shot, a $5.00 chance roughly 20%. Comparing the market's implied probability to an independent estimate is the whole basis of finding value — and it's exactly what our ratings are built to do.
