Track conditions & the going scale

The state of the ground is one of the most powerful influences on a race — and one of the most misread. Australia rates its tracks on a 1–10 scale. Here's what each number means and how it changes who wins.

The Australian track rating

Every Australian turf track is given an official rating on race day, from 1 (the firmest) to 10 (the wettest). The numbers are grouped into four named bands. A track can be upgraded or downgraded through the day as it dries out or more rain arrives.

BandRatingWhat it means
Firm1–2Hard, dry ground with little give. Rare in modern Australian racing — most clubs water to avoid it.
Good3–4The ideal racing surface: enough give to be safe, firm enough to be fast. Good 4 is the most common rating you'll see.
Soft5–7Rain-affected. The ground is holding moisture and getting slower and more testing as the number rises.
Heavy8–10Saturated, energy-sapping ground. Times slow dramatically; stamina and a genuine liking for the mud become decisive.

Why it matters so much

Horses are not equally suited to every surface. Some have an action that skims across firm ground but flounders in the wet; others revel in a bog that stops their rivals. A horse's wet-track record — its results specifically on Soft and Heavy going — is one of the most useful things you can check when rain is about. A brilliant Good-track performer with an unproven or poor record in the wet is a genuine risk on a Heavy 9, regardless of its overall class.

Reading wet-track form

When you look at a horse's past runs, note the track rating each was run on, not just the finishing position. A modest-looking third on a Heavy 10 against strong opposition can be far more telling than a win on a Good 3 in a weak field. Look for the pattern: does this horse produce its best figures when the ground softens, or does its form fall away the moment the rating climbs past 5?

The classic trap. Backing a proven, high-class horse on wet ground it has never handled. Class helps, but a Heavy track is a great leveller — it can bring a plodder with a liking for the mud right into a race it had no business being in on dry ground.

How the going changes tactics

Soft and heavy ground doesn't just slow horses down — it reshapes how races are run:

  • Leaders can be harder to catch, or much easier, depending on the track. On some heavy tracks the fence gets chopped up and on-pace runners tire; on others, being in front to avoid kickback is a big edge.
  • The best going often moves off the rail. As the inside gets churned up, the fresher ground out wide can become the place to be — which flips the usual advantage of an inside barrier draw.
  • Stamina rises in importance. Heavy ground turns a 1400m race into something that feels like 1600m; horses that stay well are favoured, and pure speed can be blunted.

A note on data quality

Track ratings are set close to race time and can change. Two horses' "Soft 6" runs may not have been on identical surfaces — ground varies across a track and through a day. Treat the rating as a strong signal, not a precise measurement, and weigh it alongside the horse's broader record rather than in isolation. Getting the going right for every runner, at race granularity, is one of the harder problems in racing analysis — and one worth taking seriously.
